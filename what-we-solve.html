The test

An AI project has to do one of six things. Otherwise it is a science experiment.

This is the filter we run every idea through before a dollar is spent. If a project cannot name which of these six it moves — and roughly by how much — it does not get built. It is the difference between AI that shows up in your financials and AI that shows up in a slide deck.

Save money

Take real cost out — labor hours, rework, overtime, third-party spend — in a number you can point at on the P&L.

Improve speed

Shorten a cycle that customers, cash flow or the next department are waiting on. Days to hours, weeks to days.

Improve accuracy

Fewer errors entering the system in the first place, so nobody downstream is cleaning up records or arguing about which number is right.

Reduce human touches

Every hand-off is a delay and a chance to introduce an error. Touching the work once beats touching it five times, even when each touch is fast.

Increase revenue

Sell more of what you already have — better listings, faster quotes, faster time to market, capacity you could not reach before.

Increase profitability

Margin, not just volume. Sometimes that is cost out and sometimes it is mix, pricing or which work you stop doing altogether.

Most of the good ones hit three or four at once. The intake workflow in our case study cut labor cost 81%, tripled throughput, dropped identification to under 30 seconds, took the process from several human touches to one, and improved the product data that drives sell-through. That is five of the six from a single project — which is what a problem worth solving usually looks like.

Our approach

We teach the finding, not just the fixing.

Most companies that need this work have never had anyone whose job was to hunt for waste — no continuous-improvement team, no Lean black belt, no internal analytics group. The friction is still there. Nobody has been asked to go looking for it.

First, how to spot it

Your team learns the pattern we look for: friction that is expensive, repetitive, measurable and solvable. Not this one instance of it — the pattern, so they recognize the next one on their own.

Then, how to fix it

How to point AI, automation or a redesigned process at what they found — and, just as important, how to tell which of the three the problem actually calls for.

Then we step back

Superusers and AI Champions who can operate and extend what was built, so the capability stays in the building when the engagement ends.

Why it works this way

Teach them to fish. Don’t hand them a caught one.

A consultant who solves the problem and leaves has sold you exactly one fish. You are back on the phone the next time something breaks. We would rather your team walk away able to find the next problem, and the one after that, without calling us.
